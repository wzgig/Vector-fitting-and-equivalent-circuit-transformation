## 4) θ：相角（相角差）波动范围——一般**没有像V那样的统一“标准固定范围”**

- 在潮流模型里，θ是相对参考母线的相角；它的“允许范围”更多由**线路潮流、稳定裕度、保护/重合闸条件、系统强弱**共同决定。  
- 一些文件会对“相角突变/大相角差”做**保护与扰动监测**类阈值，而不是日常稳态运行的“合格范围”。例如 **NERC PRC-029-1**提到与扰动相关的**正序电压相角瞬时变化超过25电角度**等条件（偏事件/扰动监视口径）。citeturn2search3  

**工程上怎么“思考θ范围”更靠谱：**
- 你可以把θ当成“P传输的结果变量”：在同一电压水平下，线路有功潮流越大、系统越弱，线路两端相角差往往越大。  
- 因此通常做法是：**用线路负载率、N-1约束、暂稳/小扰动稳定约束**反推出“相角差不应过大”，而不是反过来硬套一个全国统一θ阈值。

Impedance Model of Inverter-Based Resources
Nabil Mohammed, Senior Member, IEEE, Weihua Zhou, Member, IEEE, Behrooz Bahrani, Senior Member, IEEE,
David Hill, Life Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—The widespread integration of inverter-based re￾sources (IBRs) in modern power grids raises concerns about low￾frequency oscillations, impacting system stability and reliability
globally. The impedance model (IM) of IBRs, typically employed
for small-signal stability analysis, presents challenges due to
nonlinear dynamics and complex control schemes. Firstly, the
IM represents the inverter’s linearized dynamic model valid only
at a specific operating point, limiting its applicability across
broad operational conditions. Secondly, the limited information
provided by vendors about control structures and configurations
of commercialized IBRs hinders the accurate analytical deriva￾tion of the IM. This paper proposes a data-driven approach
using support vector machines (SVM) to create a generalized
IM for IBRs in the dq reference frame. By leveraging a small
training dataset, the algorithm demonstrates high predictive
accuracy across various operating conditions while maintaining
computational efficiency. The capability of the proposed SVM￾based IM prediction approach is demonstrated across various
IBR control configurations, including grid-following and grid￾forming inverters, eliminating the need for an extensive training
dataset. Comparative assessments with feedforward neural net￾work (FNN) modelling approach confirm the superiority of SVM,
particularly in the low-frequency range that is crucial for IBR
stability analysis. Finally, the application of the predicted IM is
demonstrated for stability analysis in the frequency domain and
validated using the electromagnetic transient (EMT) model in
the time domain through MATLAB/Simulink simulations.
Index Terms—Impedance model, inverter-based resource, op￾erating points variations, small-signal stability, support vector
machines, voltage source inverter.
I. INTRODUCTION
I
N the dynamic landscape of modern power systems, a
notable transformation is occurring wherein inverter-based
resources (IBRs) are progressively overtaking the roles tradi￾tionally held by synchronous generators [1], [2]. Consequently,
in forthcoming modern power systems, the pivotal role of
the IBRs in facilitating the transition towards enhanced sta￾bility and reliability cannot be under estimated [3]. Over the
past decade, the small-signal stability analysis approach has
emerged as an effective tool for examining the stability of
IBRs and their interactions within the grid [4].
Among the various methodologies employed for small￾signal stability analysis, the impedance-based approach holds

instability phenomena of the IBRs but also quantifies stability
margins accurately. Within the framework of impedance-based
stability analysis, two equivalent impedances are required
at a designated assessment point: the grid impedance and
output impedance of the IBR. On the one hand, the grid
impedance, traditionally considered passive, can be derived
analytically or measured in real-time using techniques like
frequency injection, pseudo-random binary sequence (PRBS),
and transient pulse methods [6]–[8].
On the other hand, the output impedance of the IBR,
denoted as the impedance model (IM), is deemed a crucial
determinant in comprehending the dynamic behavior of the
IBR [9], [10]. The IM is influenced by control loops operating
at various time scales, giving rise to instability observed across
different frequency ranges [4]. For instance, high-frequency
instability may induced by the fastest inner-current control
loop, while low-frequency instability is attributed to the impact
of slower phase-locked loop (PLL) and power control loops
[11]–[13]. Consequently, the accurate determination of the IM
for the IBR becomes imperative for an in-depth investigation
into its stability. This involves two distinct approaches: ana￾lytical methods utilizing state-space or admittance representa￾tion, necessitating detailed identification of the whole control
parameters and configurations, and identification techniques.
Deriving analytically the IM of the commercialized IBRs
used in practical applications, such as solar photovoltaic (PV)
and wind farms, is infeasible due to limited vendor disclosures
regarding the control architectures and configurations. Con￾sequently, these IBRs are treated as black boxes. Moreover,
while accurate IM for an IBR can be obtained through
identification techniques like multi-tone frequency scanning
and PRBS, the acquired IM for this black-box IBR remains
dependent on operating conditions [13]. This implies that the
measured IM of the IBR is a linearized model valid solely
within the bounds of a specific operating point (OP). When
the OP of the IBR changes, the IM needs to be remeasured,
imposing limitations on its applicability across variable OPs.
To address the challenge of OP dependency in the IBR’s IM,
recent research has explored several solutions for generating
a generalized IM under variable OPs. These approaches can
be broadly categorized as analytical model-based and data￾driven approaches. In the analytical model-based approaches,
strategies have been proposed in [14]–[16]. For instance, in
[14], a nearly-decoupled sequence domain admittance model
is devised for grid-following inverters (GFLIs). However, this
approach relies on the phase-locked loop (PLL) and lacks

Given the recent advances in the machine learning field,
data-driven techniques have gained considerable attention for
predicting IM of the IBRs under variable OPs [17]–[22]. In the
data-driven approaches, the trained IM is employed to predict
IM at new OPs. In [17], a recurrent neural network (RNN)-

OPs. In [18], an artificial neural network (ANN) is employed

[24], transfer learning-based IM predictions are proposed for
two-level and module multilevel converters. These methods
involve an offline data generation stage followed by online
impedance identification.
In [19], [21], a hybrid approach employing a small-scale
feedforward neural network (FNN) and transfer learning is
introduced for IM prediction. However, this technique can
still be intricate due to pre-training and transfer learning
requirements. In [22], a physics-informed neural network
(PINN) data-driven approach is proposed for predicting the
IM of the IBR. Despite its effectiveness in reducing the
computational load for real-time impedance identification, the
PINN necessitates both offline and online training and some
prior knowledge of IBR control structures. Furthermore, its
validity is only tested for current-controlled GFLIs. To this
end, a common concern in the aforementioned data-driven
methods is their heavy reliance on the amount of training data,
limiting their practical utility due to data constraints.
In power systems, considerable attention has been directed
towards data-driven modeling approaches over the past two
decades [25]–[27]. Among various data-driven modeling tech￾niques, the support vector machines (SVM) has garnered
significant interest, particularly in scenarios where only a
limited dataset is available for training, showcasing its inherent
accuracy. Additionally, its versatility extends to both classifi￾cation and regression tasks. Previous studies in the power sys￾tems field have demonstrated SVM’s efficacy across numerous
applications. In [28], SVM is applied for the classification of
voltage disturbances. In [29], SVM is employed to classify
various disturbances in the power system. Furthermore, in
[30], SVM is used for transient stability analysis of large￾scale grids, and in [31], SVM is utilized for fault diagnosis
in power transmission systems. Additionally, in [32], SVM
finds application in data-based line trip fault prediction in
power systems. Given its consistent superior performance
compared to other machine learning algorithms under limited
training data, there is a compelling appeal to explore SVM’s
application in modeling of GFLIs and GFMIs.
This paper proposes an efficient and accurate SVM-based
approach for predicting the IM of the IBRs. This technique
establishes a generic IM of the IBR in the dq reference frame
of any given black-box IBR. This approach accommodates
a wide range of OPs, theoretically infinite. Unlike existing
prediction methods, the proposed SVM methodology is an
efficient data-driven approach, yielding accurate impedance
predictions with a minimal training dataset.
The key contributions of this work are as follows:
1) Proposing an efficient SVM-based IM prediction ap￾proach that demands only a small OP dataset (18 OPs)
for SVM training, yet achieves accurate predictions of
the dq reference frame IM of IBRs.
2) Demonstrating the algorithm generality, enabling its
application across various IBR control configurations of
GFLIs and GFMIs, without necessitating increasing the
size of the training dataset.
3) Validating the superiority of the proposed SVM-based
IM prediction approach through a comparison with the
FNN modelling approach.
4) Employing the IM predicted by the trained SVM to
assess the stability of IBRs in the frequency domain and
validating the results in the time domain.
5) Accurately predicting the low-frequency oscillations

systems. This prediction can be leveraged to develop
mitigation solutions, including control redesigning ap￾proaches (e.g., reshaping the inverter output impedance).

C. Impacts of the Variable OPs on IM of IBRs
The low-frequency stability concerns associated with GFLIs
are primarily attributed to the qq impedance, denoted as Zqq,
which is predominantly influenced by both the dynamics of
the PLL [34] and variable OPs [13], [35]. For instance, the
resampling of a negative resistance behavior within Zqq by the
PLL leads to the initiation of the phase of Zqq from nearly
-180 degrees [34].
To further illustrate the impact of OP variations on the IM of
the IBRs, an analysis is conducted herein, with consideration
given to the current-controlled GFLI. The parameters of this
inverter are listed in Table I. Initially, the nominal currents
(corresponding to 1 p.u) are directly computed based on the
nominal active (Pn) and reactive (Qn) powers. Given that the
PLL enforces Vq to 0 for synchronization with the grid voltage
phase, the expressions Id−ref = 1.5Pref/Vd and Iq−ref =
−1.5Qref/Vd are employed. Three OPs are then considered,
corresponding to different current reference settings for Id−ref
(0, 0.5, and 1 p.u), while Iq−ref is consistently set to 0.
For each OP, the values of Zdd and Zqq are extracted from
the analytical IM of the current-controlled GFLI in the dq
reference frame, as detailed in [13].
Fig. 3 illustrates the influence of OP variations on both the
magnitude and phase angle of Zdd and Zqq for the current￾controlled GFLI. Fig. 3(a) shows that both the magnitude and
phase angle of Zdd remain unaffected by the variation in OPs.
However, as depicted in Fig. 3(b), substantial variations in both
the magnitude and phase angle of Zqq are observed across the
three considered OPs. Similar findings to this analytical IM of
the current-controlled GFLI are reported in a previous study
based on online measurements of the Zdd and Zqq, wherein
variations in OPs impact only Zqq and not Zdd of the current￾controlled GFLI [36].

A. SVM as a Modeling Tool
The SVM stands as a powerful machine learning algorithm
applicable to classification and regression tasks. These algo￾rithms belong to the family of supervised learning, particularly
demonstrating effectiveness when handling intricate datasets
featuring potential non-linear relationships between their con￾stituent features. The SVM has gained substantial prominence
due to its adeptness in managing high-dimensional data and
robustness in mitigating overfitting. The SVM algorithms serve
as vital tools in diverse domains like image recognition, text
classification, and bioinformatics [37]–[39].
For addressing linearly separable problems, the fundamental
working principle of the SVM is illustrated in Fig. 4(a). At its
core, the SVM seeks the optimal hyperplane that maximally
separates distinct classes within a dataset. This hyperplane is
positioned to maximize the margin, representing the distance
between the hyperplane and the nearest data points from each
class. The data points closest to the hyperplane, termed support
vectors, crucially contribute to defining this boundary [37].
Conversely, for resolving linearly non-separable problems
originating from real-world complexities, the SVM leverages
techniques like soft margin and kernel tricks. Soft margin
allows some misclassification, achieving a balance between
margin maximization and error minimization. Meanwhile,
kernel tricks, achieved through kernel functions as shown in
Fig. 4(b), involve transforming the feature space. This transfor￾mation empowers the SVM in delineating intricate non-linear
decision boundaries. Below is a mathematical formulation of
the SVM utilizing kernel tricks, which is employed in this
paper for the IM prediction problem.

B. SVM for Predicting the IM of IBRss
Fig. 5 presents the proposed methodology for using the
SVM algorithm to predict the IM in the dq reference frame,
Zdq. The inputs encompass the OP vector [P, Q, V ],
while the output is the IM of the IBR (the inverter output
impedance matrix Zdq) which consists of the four elements
[Zdd, Zdq, Zqd, Zqq] extracted for a specific frequency range
frange = [fmin, fmax]. The input and output vectors required
for training and testing can be obtained from perturbation mea￾surements under black-box conditions for unknown control
topologies.
The dynamic behavior of IBRs, as represented by the IM in
the frequency domain, is influenced by various factors, includ￾ing grid impedance, load variations, OP fluctuations, control
algorithms, and the interactions occurring within cascaded
control loops. The consideration of the interactions among the
aforementioned factors renders the modeling of IBR a complex
task. Consequently, the proposed SVM-based IM prediction
approach regards it as a non-separable problem, where the
utilization of the RBF kernel is employed to address this
objective.
The procedure for the SVM-based IM prediction approach
is outlined in the following steps:
• Dataset Preparation
– Choose Small OPs (N): A subset of representative
OPs is selected.
– Input Variables (X): Input variables X consist of
three vectors representing the selected OPs: X =
[P, Q, V ], each vector with a dimension of N × 1.
– Frequency Range (frange): Desired frequency range
frange = [fmin, fmax] is specified for predicting Zdq.
– Frequency of Interest (fi): The desired frequency fi
is chosen within frange.
– Output Training Dataset (Y ): Training dataset
Y includes vectors representing the IM: Z =
[Zdd, Zdq, Zqd, Zqq], each N ×1, corresponding to
chosen X at fi
. Since the output Z comprises four
vectors of complex values, they are decomposed into
real values (magnitudes and phases), resulting in a
total dimension for Y of N × 8.
• SVM Model Training
– SVM Model Training: the SVM models are trained
using the dataset (X, Y ) to map the inverter OPs to
the associated IM in the dq reference frame at the
specified frequency fi
.
– Frequency Variation: Repeat model training for dif￾ferent fi within frange, keeping the input vector X
constant.
• SVM Model Validation
– Validation of Prediction Accuracy: Validate the pre￾diction accuracy using the dedicated testing dataset.
• Impedance Prediction for New Inputs
– Use the trained SVM models to predict new IM Ynew
for any input vector Xnew = [Pnew Qnew Vnew]. This
enables the estimation of the IM of the IBR under
variable conditions without the need to derive the
analytical model or re-measure the IM.

D. Comparison with the FNN Algorithm
In order to validate the superiority of the proposed SVM
approach for predicting the IM of IBRs, a comparative study is
conducted in this section with the conventional FNN approach.
The architecture of the FNN employed in this comparative
analysis consists of 15 hidden layers, each comprising 10 neu￾rons. The FNN employs hyperbolic tangent sigmoid transfer
functions.
Fig. 13(a), (b), and (c) presents a comprehensive comparison
between true impedance values and predicted results from the
seven random tests conducted at three distinct frequencies:
0.1 Hz, 1 Hz, and 5 Hz. In this figure, the true IMs are also
plotted with the predicted ones obtained for the two data￾driven algorithms: the FNN and the proposed SVM.
The results depicted in Fig. 13(a) illustrate significant errors
in the predictions made by the FNN algorithm at the low
frequency of 0.1 Hz. These pronounced errors can be attributed
to the limited dataset utilized for training the prediction models
and to the high impedance dynamics associated with variations
in the OP within this frequency range. Improvements in
prediction accuracy for the FNN method are evident at 1
Hz, as depicted in Fig. 13(b), with further accuracy enhance￾ments observed at 5 Hz, as shown in Fig. 13(c). Despite
these improvements, noticeable errors remain. Conversely, the
results presented in Fig. 13 demonstrate that the proposed
SVM algorithm accurately predicts the IM across all tested
frequencies: 0.1 Hz, 1 Hz, and 5 Hz.
It is noteworthy that alternative architectures for the FNN
are examined throughout the investigation. However, irre￾spective of the various architectures considered, significantly
lower accuracy is consistently demonstrated by the FNN in
comparison to the SVM approach. These findings are primarily
attributable to the underlying principles of both approaches
when a limited size of the dataset is available for the training
process [41]. Furthermore, comparing the performance of the
SVM-based IM prediction approach with other NN-based
approaches would lead to similar conclusions as observed
with the FNN. This tendency is attributed to the working
principles of NN-based approaches, which generally exhibit
lower accuracy in prediction or regression problems when
trained with small amounts of data in comparison to the SVM
approach [44], [45].

GFMI. Its application for impedance-based stability analysis
of systems with one and multiple IBRs will be elaborated in
the upcoming subsection.

V. CONCLUSIONS
This paper proposes an SVM-based data-driven approach
to generate a generalized IM for any IBR in the dq reference
frame. The approach demonstrates accurate IM prediction with
a small training dataset of 18 operating points, offering value
for IBR modeling. The accuracy of the proposed modelling
approach is extensively assessed by comparing predicted IMs
to analytical IMs for different grid-following and grid-forming
inverters. Results highlight the capability of the SVM-based
IM prediction approach across various control configurations
of the IBRs, obviating the need for additional training data.
Comparative analysis against the FNN modeling approach
confirms the superiority of the SVM-based approach, espe￾cially in predicting low-frequency dynamic behavior. Further￾more, the predicted IM is effectively utilized for stability as￾sessment in the frequency domain and validated in the time do￾main using an EMT simulation model of a current-controlled
grid-following inverter implemented in MATLAB/Simulink.
Overall, the contributions of this article lie in the efficient
development of the SVM-based IM prediction approach, its
adaptability to various control configurations, and its predictive
accuracy in the low-frequency range, all of which contribute to
advancing IBRs modeling, thus enhancing power grid stability
analysis.

### C. 无功 Q（或功率因数约束）
风电的 Q 建议不要像论文那样简单给 \([-1,1]\) p.u“一刀切”，而要**贴合“能力曲线 + 功率因数要求”**。

**常规/并网要求导向（推荐）**
- 许多工程要求风机/场站功率因数可在 **超前 0.95 到 滞后 0.95** 动态可调。citeturn1search1turn1search5  
  由此可得到一个很实用的“随 P 变化”的无功边界：
  \[
  |Q| \le P \tan(\arccos(0.95)) \approx 0.329\,P
  \]
  （这里假设以同一基准归一化，且在恒 PF 模式下近似成立）

**扩展/控制能力覆盖（用于建模更稳）**
- 若你想覆盖“电压控制模式（Q 跟随 V）/SVG/SVC 配合”的更大无功调节，可在低 P 时允许更大 |Q|（因为电流裕度更大），用一个“能力圆”更贴近逆变器物理：
  \[
  P^2 + Q^2 \le S_{\max}^2
  \]
  其中 \(S_{\max}\) 取 1 p.u（以并网变流器额定视在功率为基准）。这会自然得到：P 越低，Q 可越大；P 越高，Q 越被“挤压”。

**落地建议（最容易用来采样/建数据集）**
- 对每个 P 采样点，取 3 个 Q 水平：  
  \[
  Q \in \{-Q_{\max}(P),\ 0,\ +Q_{\max}(P)\}
  \]
  其中 \(Q_{\max}(P)\) 取“功率因数约束”和“能力圆约束”两者的更小值。  
这样既像论文一样“少点覆盖面大”，又不会采到风电做不到的 (P,Q) 组合。

### D. 相角 θ（你特别点名的参数）
严格说：**稳态潮流里 θ 是“电压相角”状态量**；而论文里的阻抗建模在 dq 坐标下通常默认参考系跟随 PLL/同步角，稳态 \(V_q=0\)，所以 θ 往往**不作为独立输入**。但如果你希望把 θ 显式纳入（例如做潮流/短路强度变化下的等值、或把“相角差/功角”当作工况维度），可以这样设：

**常规/连续（稳态、不过载、不接近极限传输）**
- \(\theta \in [-10^\circ, +10^\circ]\)（并网点到强网等值源的相角差通常不会太大）

**扩展/弱网与大功率传输（用于鲁棒性）**
- \(\theta \in [-30^\circ, +30^\circ]\)

不建议再大很多，因为大相角差往往意味着你已经接近线路/等值传输极限（或者等值模型假设不再合理）。

> 另外，如果你关心的是“PLL 动态对阻抗（尤其 Zqq）的影响”，论文明确指出低频稳定性与 PLL、以及工况变化相关。 fileciteturn1file8L109-L116
